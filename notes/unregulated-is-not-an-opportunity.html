An unregulated market is not an opportunity

I spent the opening weeks of a project arguing we should target the markets where crypto asset law had not arrived yet. Reading the statutes changed my mind. The reason is more useful than the conclusion.

The product was a stablecoin escrow for cross border trade, aimed at importers and exporters too small to get a Letter of Credit priced sensibly. Picking target markets was mine to work out, and my first instinct was to follow the trade volumes. India moves an enormous quantity of goods across borders. It also had, at the time, no framework for smart contract enforceability, no regulatory route for issuing or handling stablecoins, and a flat tax on virtual digital assets sitting where a licensing regime would normally be.

I read that as a gap. Large market, no rules yet, first mover advantage. It is a tempting shape of argument because it sounds like risk appetite rather than carelessness.

What the reading actually showed

The mistake is in the phrase "no rules yet". There are two very different situations that both look like an absence of regulation from a distance.

In the first, a regulator has looked at an activity and decided it sits outside the perimeter, or has set out how it intends to bring it inside. There is a published position, and you can build against it. In the second, nobody has decided anything. There is no licence to apply for, no supervisor to register with, no test you can pass and point to afterwards.

The second one is not a light touch environment. It is an environment where you cannot demonstrate compliance even if you are, in substance, compliant. A bank will not open an account for you on the strength of your own opinion of yourself. Nor will a counterparty accept your escrow as a substitute for their bank's undertaking. The entire product depended on being credible to people who had every reason to be sceptical, and there was no mechanism in that market by which we could become credible.

You cannot demonstrate compliance in a market that has not decided what compliance means.

The other failure mode

Turkey looked different on the surface and failed for a related reason. It had a rule, and the rule was a central bank prohibition on using crypto assets for payments. That is at least a clear answer, and it is a no. A clear no is genuinely more useful than silence, because it takes ten minutes to act on rather than ten weeks.

We narrowed to the United Kingdom, the European Union and the United Arab Emirates. Not because they are permissive, and in places they are noticeably stricter, but because each of them is actively building law around stablecoins and crypto assets. There is something to read, someone to apply to, and a defined thing to become.

What I would carry into any market entry question

  • Market size does not offset regulatory silence. They are not the same unit and they do not trade against each other.
  • The useful question is not "is this allowed" but "what is the mechanism by which I could prove it is allowed, to a bank, a counterparty and a court".
  • If that mechanism does not exist, the market is closed regardless of how open it feels.
  • Narrowing early is not conservatism. Three jurisdictions analysed properly beat six analysed at the depth of a summary, and the difference shows immediately in the quality of the register.

The last part is the bit I would tell myself at the start. I was reluctant to cut markets because cutting felt like shrinking the ambition of the project. It did the opposite. The analysis that survived was the only part anyone could act on.
