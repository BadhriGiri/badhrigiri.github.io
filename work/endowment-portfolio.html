Endowment portfolio

Investment recommendations for a university endowment fund under budget pressure, with a target of 0.3% monthly return. I was the team's lead quantitative analyst, which meant owning the optimisation work and then saying where it should not be trusted.

The brief

A return target and no room to be wrong

A university endowment fund under budget pressure needed a multi asset allocation that would deliver 0.3% a month. The number matters more than it looks. An endowment under pressure cannot absorb a bad year the way a fund with headroom can, so the brief was really about the downside as much as the return.

Six of us split the work. I ran the optimisation and the testing, which meant I was the one who had to say whether the allocation we were about to recommend would survive contact with data the model had never seen.

What I built

The allocation, and the curve underneath it

Mean variance optimisation and efficient frontier analysis to find the allocations worth considering, then a Black Litterman overlay so the team's own views on the market could be folded in without letting them overwhelm the historical estimates. Yield curves built by bootstrapping and Nelson Siegel fitting, with bond valuation sitting on top of them. Performance judged on return, volatility, Sharpe ratio and maximum drawdown rather than return alone.

None of that is unusual. It is the standard toolkit, applied carefully. What follows is the part I would actually point at.

Where I disagreed with the model

Three times the output was not the recommendation

  1. It wanted 84.9% of the fund in one strategy

    The optimiser is indifferent to concentration risk unless you tell it not to be. It put almost the entire allocation into a single strategy because that is what the historical numbers rewarded. I flagged it and recommended a 50% cap. The capped portfolio is worse on paper and better in practice, and for a fund that cannot absorb a bad year that trade is the right way round.

  2. The expected returns were built on too little data

    Mean variance optimisation is notoriously sensitive to its expected return inputs, and ours were estimated from 96 observations. I tested how much the allocation moved when the sample length changed, found it moved a great deal, and re-ran the analysis on 150 observations to stabilise the inputs. A model whose answer depends on how far back you started looking is not giving you an answer.

  3. The in sample result was not the result

    I rebuilt the statistical arbitrage hedge ratio on a rolling 252 day window rather than fitting it once over the whole period, and added estimation bias analysis. Out of sample, the Sharpe ratio went from 0.41 to 0.56 and maximum drawdown fell from 1.23% to 0.98%. An in sample result that does not survive out of sample testing is not a result, it is a description of the past.

Where it is unfinished

What I would want to check before anyone acted on it

  • The 50% cap is a judgment, not an optimisation. It is defensible and it is not derived from anything. A real mandate would set concentration limits as a constraint agreed with the client up front, not as a correction applied afterwards.
  • Transaction costs and rebalancing frequency are not modelled. A rolling window strategy trades, and trading is not free. The out of sample figures would come down.
  • This was an assessed university project worked on over one term, not a live mandate. The analysis is careful and it has not been tested by anybody losing money on it.
