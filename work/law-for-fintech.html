Law for FinTech

An essay arguing that culture, governance and compliance are not three subjects but three expressions of a single question: do people here follow the rules when nobody is watching?

The thesis

Adequate rules, inadequately lived

The usual reading of financial scandal is that the rules were too weak and more of them would have helped. The essay argues the opposite: from Lehman Brothers through Wirecard to Silicon Valley Bank, the rules were largely adequate and were not followed. The failures were cultural first. Culture corrupted governance, and corrupted governance made compliance a paperwork exercise.

That matters practically, because the two diagnoses lead to opposite remedies. If the problem is rules, you write more of them. If the problem is whether anybody intends to follow them, more rules add cost and change nothing.

Culture

The gap between the stated and the lived

Every firm has a stated culture and a real one, and the distance between them is the thing worth measuring. I used my own accounting internship as the case rather than a textbook example, because I was actually there for it.

The firm's stated values were honesty, hard work and teamwork, and those were broadly real. What was missing was traceability: it was not always possible to connect a given piece of work back to the named person who did it. That is precisely what the Senior Managers and Certification Regime exists to force in regulated firms, and I raised it with the partners rather than writing it up afterwards. The deficiency was in the documentation infrastructure, not in anybody's intent, which is usually how these gaps actually look from the inside.

Governance and compliance

Structures that hold, and rules followed in spirit

Governance maturity is not whether the committees exist. It is whether they hold under pressure, which is the only time anybody finds out. A board that functions in a good quarter and is bypassed in a bad one did not have governance, it had an org chart.

Compliance gets treated as binary, compliant or not, when it actually runs on a spectrum from technical conformity to genuine alignment with what the rule was for. Deadline pressure is where firms slide down that spectrum, and they rarely notice, because nothing registers as a breach.

The FCA now assesses culture as a leading indicator of consumer harm, which is the regulator agreeing with the essay's premise: a firm with impressive governance architecture and a blame avoidant interior is a higher risk than its paperwork suggests.
