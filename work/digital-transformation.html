The case

Ten years, reconstructed from primary sources

The first half was a timeline of Lloyds' digital evolution from 2016 to 2026, built by going through annual reports, industry publications and press releases rather than secondary summaries. Digital foundations first, then cloud migration and data modernisation, then AI acceleration, then enterprise integration under a digital programme of around £4 billion.

The scale is the thing that makes it interesting as a case. Roughly 8,000 technology hires and more than 80 machine learning use cases in deployment, inside an institution that still has to run a bank while it rebuilds one.

I deliberately did not use generative AI for this part. The whole portfolio rested on these facts being right, and I wanted a foundation I had verified myself before doing anything analytical on top of it.

The analysis

Five challenges, and the one that is actually hard

The consultant piece identified five: legacy system integration, talent acquisition at that volume, regulatory compliance across Consumer Duty and the EU AI Act, change management, and competitive pressure from challengers and big tech.

Four of those are expensive. AI governance is the one that is genuinely hard, because it is not solved by spending more. Deploying eighty models into a regulated institution means being able to explain any individual decision one of them made, to a customer or to a regulator, possibly years later. The recommendation was phased legacy modernisation on a cloud first architecture with parallel running, which is slower and far less likely to take the bank down.

The reflection

What this says about where I want to work

The portfolio closed with a career pathway piece on working at the intersection of model deployment, AI governance and business impact inside a regulated institution. That is the same seam the compliance work on Blockmediary runs along: the interesting problems are not in the technology or in the rules, they are in the join between them.
