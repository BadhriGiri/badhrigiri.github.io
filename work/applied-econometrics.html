University of Exeter · MSc project · R

Applied Econometrics

A panel study of household savings behaviour in the United Kingdom: 540 observations across 90 individuals over six waves. I specified the model before opening the data, then spent most of the project arguing with it.

  • Panel data
  • Fixed effects
  • Hypothesis testing
  • R
Format
Assessed MSc project, individual work
Built in
R, using the plm package
Data
540 observations, 90 individuals, six waves
Method
Two-way fixed effects with HC1 robust standard errors

The approach

Specify first, then look

I wrote the specification down before opening the data and argued each variable in on its own merits. That ordering matters more in econometrics than almost anywhere else, because a panel of 540 observations will happily produce a significant coefficient for something if you go looking for one after the fact.

The model used two-way fixed effects, absorbing both individual level and year level variation, so the estimates come from how a given household's savings move over time rather than from differences between households. Standard errors were HC1 robust, because the assumption of constant variance across households is not one I was willing to make.

What it found

Four results and one non-result

  1. Income moves savings, but not one for one

    A one pound increase in monthly income was associated with roughly seventy pence of additional savings, significant at the 5% level. The gap between the pound and the seventy pence is the interesting part, and it is where consumption goes.

  2. Households with children saved more

    Around £3,885 more, significant at the 10% level. Weaker evidence than the income result, and I reported it at the level it actually reached rather than rounding the confidence up.

  3. Savings revert to the mean

    The coefficient on previous year savings was negative, at -0.137, and strongly significant. A good saving year tends to be followed by a weaker one, which is what you would expect if households are smoothing toward a target rather than accumulating steadily.

  4. The non-result I kept

    I tested quadratic and cubic income terms with an F-test and they were not significant, so I dropped them and kept the linear specification. Added complexity that does not earn its place makes a model harder to defend and easier to overfit. Reporting the test rather than quietly removing the terms is the point.

Policy

A difference in differences estimate

The final piece used difference in differences to estimate the effect of a government savings incentive of £500, comparing year one, before the programme, against year four as it wound down. The point estimate came out at about £1,065 of additional household savings.

It is not statistically significant. The p value is 0.38, so the honest reading is that this design, on 180 observations, cannot distinguish the programme's effect from nothing. I am reporting the number because it is what the model produced, and reporting the p value because a point estimate without one invites a reader to believe something the data does not support.

The joint test on the time fixed effects was a different matter and did come through, F = 3.52 at p = 0.0039, so there is genuine variation across periods for an estimator to work with. The limitation is the design rather than the data.

A difference in differences estimate also rests on the parallel trends assumption, which is an assumption and not a finding. If treated and untreated households were already diverging before the programme, the estimate absorbs that divergence.
