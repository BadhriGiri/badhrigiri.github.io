Forecasting and smart contracts

Three technical pieces in Python, each with a different way of being wrong. An optimiser that would not converge, a price forecast that could easily have cheated, and a contract that had to work on a chain where you cannot quietly try again.

The brief

Build the thing, then say why you believe it

The written half of the module asked how blockchain systems should be evaluated and compared against each other: signature schemes and their resilience to quantum attack, consensus mechanisms, and the difference between ledger state models. The practical half asked for working code.

What ties the three pieces below together is that each one can produce a confident looking output that is worthless, and in each case the interesting work was finding out whether that had happened.

01

Gradient descent, written from first principles

Rather than calling a library, the exercise was to implement gradient descent myself and fit a relationship between Bitcoin and Dogecoin price data. It did not converge. The loss simply refused to settle, which is the least informative failure a model can give you, because almost anything can cause it.

The cause turned out to be the data rather than the algorithm. Bitcoin and Dogecoin are separated by several orders of magnitude in price, so a single learning rate could not suit both series at once: the step that was reasonable for one was wildly wrong for the other. Normalising the inputs fixed it.

The part worth keeping is what came after. Gradient descent on a linear relationship has a closed form solution, so I solved it analytically and checked that my implementation landed in the same place. Without that check I would have had a model that converged, which is not the same thing as a model that is right.

02

Forecasting Ethereum without cheating

Daily price data pulled from an API, eleven lagged features engineered from it, and an ElasticNet model fitted on top. The modelling is routine. The two decisions around it are not.

The first is cross validation. Ordinary k fold cross validation shuffles the data, which on a time series means training on next week to predict last week. The score that comes out looks excellent and means nothing. I used time ordered cross validation so the model was only ever tested on data that came after what it had been trained on.

The second is the benchmark. A price forecast should be compared against the naive prediction that tomorrow looks like today, because a surprising number of models cannot beat it. I evaluated on R squared and mean absolute error against that benchmark rather than reporting the model's own accuracy in isolation. Reporting a number with nothing to compare it to is how forecasts get oversold.

03

A contract that had to run in public

The third piece was a dictator game, the behavioural economics experiment where one participant divides a sum and the other has no say, implemented as a smart contract, tested, and deployed to a live chain with a browser front end so it could actually be played.

Deploying changes the standard. A contract on a live chain is immutable and public: there is no quietly patching a mistake, and anybody can read what you wrote. That is a useful thing to have experienced directly before writing about why the immutability of a ledger creates problems elsewhere, which is exactly what the compliance work on Blockmediary ran into later.

Where it is unfinished

What this is and is not

  • These are assessed university exercises, not production systems. They were built to demonstrate that I understand the methods, and they have not been run against money or load.
  • The Ethereum forecast is an exercise in evaluating a model honestly, not a claim that crypto prices can be predicted. Treating it as the latter would be the exact mistake the benchmark was there to prevent.
  • The contract was audited by nobody. It is small and it is a teaching example, and I would not deploy it anywhere that mattered without a review.
