Fundamentals of Financial Technology

Four exercises in sequence, each one harder to fake than the last: read a live blockchain, measure what Bitcoin actually is as an asset, reproduce somebody else's model, then build your own.

  • Bitcoin
  • CAPM
  • Machine learning
  • APIs
Format
Assessed MSc project, individual work
Built in
Python, plus a browser wallet in HTML, CSS and JavaScript
Data
Live blockchain API, FRED, market price series
Ends with
A credit default classifier built from scratch

01

Reading a blockchain rather than reading about one

The first exercise pulled a real transaction from the Bitcoin blockchain through its API and took the block apart field by field, including converting the timestamp out of Unix epoch and explaining what each component is actually there for. I then built a browser based wallet that generates a key pair locally, derives an address, renders it as a QR code and pulls live prices from a market API.

The wallet stores nothing and generates a new key on every page load, which makes it useless as a wallet and exactly right as a teaching artefact. The point was understanding how an address comes to exist, not building something anyone should put money in.

02

What Bitcoin is, measured rather than asserted

Three series pulled from FRED and elsewhere: Bitcoin as the risky asset, gold as the safe one, and the S&P 500 as the benchmark. Prices converted to log returns, then correlation analysis and a CAPM regression producing alpha, beta and R squared for each.

This is the exercise that answers the question people argue about rhetorically. Beta tells you whether Bitcoin amplifies market moves, and R squared tells you how much of it the market explains at all. A low R squared is itself a finding: it means most of what is happening is not coming from the market factor, which is a problem for anyone describing the asset purely as leveraged equity risk.

03

Reproducing before building

Before building anything of my own I had to reproduce a published machine learning model from its written description, working out the structure, the feature pipeline and the role of logistic regression in it, then explain why logistic regression suits a credit decision where ordinary least squares does not.

The answer is that a credit decision is a probability bounded between zero and one, and a linear model will cheerfully predict a negative default probability. Reproducing someone else's work first is also the fastest way to find out whether you understood the paper or only recognised the words in it.

04

A credit default classifier of my own

The final piece was my own classifier, evaluated on accuracy, ROC-AUC and a full classification report, with the confusion matrix read in business terms rather than statistical ones. A false negative is a borrower who defaults and was approved. A false positive is a profitable customer turned away.

Those two errors are not equally expensive, and the threshold is a commercial decision rather than a modelling one. Presenting it that way is the difference between handing a lender a model and handing them something they can actually set policy with.
