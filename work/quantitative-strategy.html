University of Exeter · MSc individual project · BEAM046

Quantitative strategy integration

A solo report for a portfolio management team, integrating four active strategies into an equity book. The optimiser's preferred answer was a concentrated bet I recommended capping, and the strategy it liked best was built on a hedge ratio that drifts.

  • Nelson Siegel
  • Statistical arbitrage
  • AR model selection
  • Out of sample testing
Format
Assessed individual report, BEAM046 Financial Modelling
Client
Axiom Alpha portfolio management team, a teaching case
Strategies
Yield curve regime switching, momentum, dual listing arbitrage
Headline
Out of sample Sharpe 0.41 to 0.56, maximum drawdown 1.23% to 0.98%

The brief

Four strategies, and whether they earn their place

Starting from a mean variance optimal equity portfolio and asking what adding active strategies actually does to it. The baseline is instructive on its own: it had an in sample Sharpe of 0.41 which decayed to 0.29 out of sample, a textbook case of optimisation error, where the model fits the past rather than the process generating it.

Everything after that is an argument about whether a strategy survives contact with data it has not seen.

What I built

A regime switcher, a momentum rule, an arbitrage

A Nelson Siegel yield curve model driving a regime switcher, which reached an out of sample Sharpe of 0.48 against the equity baseline's 0.29. An AR model for momentum, with the lag order chosen on AIC rather than by eye: AR(2) at -9374.38 against AR(1) at -9372.95, a narrow margin that I reported as narrow. And a dual listing statistical arbitrage on Shell's two listings, where the cointegration test came back decisively (DF = -28.94, p = 0.000).

Combined, the nine asset portfolio reached an in sample Sharpe of 0.93 and an out of sample Sharpe of 0.61, with maximum drawdown an order of magnitude below the equity only alternatives.

Where I disagreed with the model

Two corrections

  1. It wanted 84.9% of the book in one strategy

    The optimiser allocated 84.90% to the Shell statistical arbitrage, because it had the highest in sample Sharpe and the lowest standard deviation. Mean variance is indifferent to concentration risk unless you tell it otherwise, and it will happily maximally exploit a single near Gaussian return series.

    My recommendation was to adopt the combined portfolio with a practical cap of 50% on the arbitrage weight. That portfolio is worse on paper. It is also the one I would be willing to defend if the spread relationship stopped behaving.

  2. The hedge ratio was fixed, and the relationship was not

    The original strategy used a single in sample hedge ratio of 1.0206. Two listings of the same company drift apart for ordinary reasons: currency, liquidity, dividend timing. A constant ratio quietly assumes none of that happens.

    I re-estimated it daily on a rolling 252 day window, computing z scores from that window's own mean and standard deviation. Out of sample, Sharpe went from 0.41 to 0.56 and maximum drawdown from 1.23% to 0.98%. The improvement being larger out of sample than in sample is the part that matters, because it indicates the fix removed a genuine misspecification rather than fitting the noise more closely.

Where it is unfinished

What I would want checked

  • The 50% cap is a judgment, not an optimisation. It is defensible and it is not derived from anything. A real mandate would agree concentration limits with the client up front as a constraint, rather than applying a correction afterwards.
  • A rolling re-estimation trades more than a fixed one, and transaction costs are not in these figures. The out of sample improvement would shrink.
  • Statistical arbitrage on a cointegrated pair works until the cointegration breaks, and nothing in this report tells you when that will happen.
