Intermediation

Four institutions, four different transformations

Intermediaries exist because markets are imperfect: they cut search and transaction costs and they transform things. The useful framing is that each institution type performs a different transformation, and is therefore broken by a different shock. Commercial banks do maturity transformation, funding long loans with short deposits. Insurers pool risk. Pension funds smooth consumption across decades. Mutual funds do denomination and diversification.

Rising rates hit each differently. US commercial bank net interest margins widened from 2.54% to 3.31% between early 2022 and late 2023 as loans repriced faster than deposits, which looks like good news until you reach the other side of the balance sheet.

The example that does the work

Silicon Valley Bank

SVB is the cleanest illustration available of maturity mismatch becoming existential. A fixed income portfolio of around $91 billion accumulated during the low rate era, and mark to market losses that exceeded the bank's entire equity capital once yields rose.

Nothing about that was hidden or exotic. It was the ordinary business of banking, done at a scale and a duration that left no room for rates to move. Insurers and pension funds took comparable mark to market losses as the UK ten year gilt went from 0.8% to 4.6%, but their liabilities are long too, so higher yields actually improved the present value coverage of what they owe. Same shock, opposite consequence, because the liability structure differs.

Information and incentives

Adverse selection, moral hazard, and a £38 billion bill

Adverse selection happens before the contract and moral hazard after it, and almost every control in retail finance is aimed at one or the other. Credit screening, collateral and covenants address the first. Deductibles, co-insurance and no claims discounts address the second.

Where the information advantage runs the other way, the institution is the hazard. The UK payment protection insurance scandal produced roughly £38 billion in compensation by 2022, which is what exploiting an information asymmetry costs when it is eventually priced. The FCA Consumer Duty is the regulatory answer: it converts acting in the client's interest from an ethical preference into an obligation.

Transmission and the limits of it

Why the Bank Rate took so long to bite

The Bank of England took Bank Rate from 0.1% to 5.25% against CPI inflation that peaked at 11.1%, transmitting through interest rates, bank lending, asset prices, the exchange rate and expectations. Quantitative tightening unwound an asset purchase programme of around £895 billion alongside it.

The part worth noticing is the lag. Most UK mortgage holders were on fixed rate contracts, so the policy did not reach household disposable income for a long time. A transmission mechanism that depends on contract structure is not a lever with a predictable delay, and that is a limitation of the tool rather than a failure to use it properly.

Green finance

The same problem from two very different starting points

The UK side is a disclosure and incentive story: TCFD aligned climate risk disclosure mandated by the PRA and FCA, the Pension Schemes Act 2021 requiring climate risk assessment, and commercial initiatives such as Lloyds reducing rates on energy efficiency lending.

India is doing serious work too, with around $55.9 billion of cumulative sustainable debt issuance, sovereign green bonds establishing a domestic yield curve, and the RBI green deposits framework. But it faces a constraint the UK does not: extending affordable energy to 1.4 billion people and decarbonising at speed are in genuine tension with each other. Treating that as a failure of ambition rather than a real policy conflict is the standard mistake in this literature.
