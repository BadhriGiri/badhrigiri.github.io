Recognition

Most Innovative Idea, FinTech West 2026

Blockmediary won Most Innovative Idea in the group category at the fifth annual FinTech West Student Talent Showcase, held at RSM UK's Bristol office in September 2026 and judged by Louis Clark and Noel Helliwell of Lloyds Banking Group. Nine students presented across the University of Bristol, UWE Bristol and Exeter. It was the first year Exeter was invited.

Only two people per team were allowed on stage, so Tamer Mostafa and Mohammed Reshi delivered the pitch. The rest of us took the judges' questions in the Q and A straight after it.

FinTech West's write up of the event said this about the presentation:

“The team has already completed an end-to-end settlement using real funds on a public blockchain.” FinTech West, event write up

A testnet transaction can be rerun until it behaves. A settlement on a public chain happens once, stays there, and anybody can go and read it. The sign off between the two was mine to give, which meant anything I had got wrong in the risk register would have been live, in public, and attached to my name.

The brief

A trust problem that pricing has not solved

The Letter of Credit is the instrument that makes cross border trade possible between two parties who have no reason to trust each other. A bank stands in the middle: the seller ships knowing a bank has committed to pay, and the buyer pays knowing the goods have been documented. It works, and for a small importer it is slow, expensive and frequently out of reach. The result is a standoff. Sellers will not ship before payment is secured, and buyers will not pay before goods arrive.

Blockmediary replaces the issuing bank with a smart contract. The buyer locks stablecoin in a non custodial escrow, the seller ships and uploads the trade documents, a deterministic rules engine grades those documents against the terms both parties agreed, and the escrow releases the funds. Both sides can see the state of the deal throughout.

Our team built it as the final piece of the MSc Financial Technology programme, in place of a traditional dissertation. Six of us split the work across product research, smart contracts, backend, frontend and compliance: Tamer Mostafa, Conrad Beckman, Mohammed Reshi, Daniel Price, Nick Cheng and me. The pages that follow cover my half of it, which was the legal and regulatory workstream.

Why compliance was not a final step

The right to erasure under GDPR runs directly into the immutability of a blockchain. You cannot resolve that by writing a policy at the end. It has to be designed for, which is why I was reading architecture decisions from week one rather than reviewing them at the finish.

What I owned

Four deliverables, and the reasoning under each

Nothing here was assigned as a template to fill in. Each one started from a blank page and a set of primary sources.

  1. A legal risk register, built from scratch

    The first substantive thing I produced, and it ran to twenty logged risks. It covered regulatory exposure across stablecoin issuance, payment services, anti money laundering and business verification, data privacy and trade finance law, in the United Kingdom, the European Union and the United Arab Emirates. It grew through the project to take in export controls, the money laundering chain created by platform initiated deals, and the governance question of who is allowed to pause the contract and on what grounds.

  2. The Trade Escrow Agreement

    The legal instrument that sits underneath the smart contract, because code that moves money is not self sufficient as a contract. I drafted definitions, funding mechanics, document submission, the objection window, dispute resolution through ICC DOCDEX, liability and data protection. Writing it is what taught me that most of a contract is deciding what happens when the happy path does not.

  3. Jurisdiction and licensing analysis

    Which markets we could realistically operate in, and under whose supervision. This covered the trade offs between a mainland licence that reaches the whole UAE market at a much higher first year cost, and a financial free zone route under common law with lower capital requirements and direct access to international banks. My job was to verify the licensing and cost detail before the team built a strategy on top of it.

  4. Competitor analysis and the domain standards

    I benchmarked five competitors, including a well funded predecessor that shut down in 2023, because a failure is stronger evidence that a market gap is real than a feature comparison is. Separately I wrote up UCP 600 and Incoterms 2020 as proper reference documents, since our rules engine had to be checked against the actual standards rather than a shorthand version of them.

The decision I reversed

Two calls, one wrong at the start and one wrong at the end

At proposal stage I wanted to use India's undeveloped position on crypto assets to our advantage. The reasoning felt sound: import and export volumes were large enough that the market opportunity should outweigh the legal uncertainty. India had no framework for smart contract enforceability, no regulatory route for stablecoins, and a flat tax on virtual digital assets with nothing resembling a licensing regime behind it.

That was the wrong read, and reading the law properly is what changed it. An undeveloped legal landscape is not a grey area to move into. It is the absence of any established path to compliance, and no amount of underlying trade volume compensates for that. Saudi Arabia failed on the same test, and Turkey had a central bank prohibition on using crypto assets for payments. We narrowed to the United Kingdom, the European Union and the United Arab Emirates. The register reached a depth it would never have reached spread across six markets.

An undeveloped legal landscape is not a gap in the market. It is the absence of a route to compliance.

The second reversal ran the other way, and it landed in the final week. Our standing recommendation since June had been one free zone regulator. New information gathered from contacts on the ground changed the picture, and three of us agreed to reverse it to a partner led pilot in a different free zone running alongside an independent virtual asset licence application.

That was uncomfortable to absorb that close to a deadline, and it is also the clearest evidence that the compliance work was load bearing rather than decorative. Reversing the recommendation meant rewriting the risk register, the risk assessment document, the custody clause of the Trade Escrow Agreement and the competitor analysis so that all four agreed with each other, inside a single day. Documents that quietly contradict each other going into submission are worse than documents that are merely incomplete.

Sign off

The demo is a real transaction, not a simulation

The team had agreed in July that attempting one genuine transaction was worth pursuing. Mine was the final compliance sign off before real money moved on Base Mainnet rather than a testnet.

I went into that conversation sceptical, and expecting it to be adversarial in the way legal and engineering conversations are supposed to be. It was not. We talked constantly about what they wanted to do, I checked each piece against the compliance picture as I understood it, and I was able to clear almost everything they needed before they executed. The settlement in our final deck is verifiable on a public block explorer. That outcome was better than the best case I wrote down for myself at the start of the project, and it happened because the collaboration worked, not because I lowered the bar.

Ahead of it, I also wrote plain English guidance into the repository on handling private keys and environment files. I chose documentation over an automated pre commit hook once it was clear the two people executing the transaction already understood the risk, which is the kind of judgment call I would rather make explicitly than default on.

Where it is unfinished

What I would tell an investor before they asked

I wrote the compliance set as an academic exercise that takes the shape of a real one. It is scaffolding for what would need to be built, and I would rather say so here than have it found.

  • The custody classification in the Trade Escrow Agreement is deliberately left unresolved. Whether a non custodial escrow contract creates a custody relationship in each target jurisdiction is a question I flagged rather than answered.
  • The conflict between an on chain specification hash and the right to erasure is recorded as an open item, not a solved one.
  • Our model names third party document examiners as a requirement, and there is no vendor standing behind that requirement yet. Selecting one would be the first thing I did if we kept building.
  • Whether any of this continues past the hackathon is genuinely open. One of the founders is in early conversations in the UAE about taking it further, and it may come to nothing. I mention it because the free zone licensing work in this case study was written for exactly that decision, which is why it was worth doing properly rather than notionally.

Blockmediary's whole pitch is replacing a trusted institution with something a person can check for themselves. A compliance document that overstates its own completeness would undercut exactly that.
